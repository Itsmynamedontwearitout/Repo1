# Mealybugs

## How to recognise them

- White, cottony or waxy blobs, 2–5 mm long, often clustered where stems join, along leaf midribs, in the crown and on the roots.
- Sticky leaves and surfaces. This is honeydew, the sugary liquid they give off, and black sooty mould can grow on it.
- Yellowing, curling or browning leaves, and stunted new growth.
- Ants on a plant often mean mealybugs, because ants protect them for the honeydew.
- On ferns, the lookalike is fern scale: white flecks and brown shell-like bumps along the veins that don't wipe off and don't make honeydew. The treatment is the same.

## Why plant sensors miss them

Sensors like Willow report growing conditions, so a plant can read as "happy" while it's infested. Mealybugs like warm, still, sheltered indoor spots, which are the same spots plants do well in. They usually arrive on a new nursery plant or spread from a neighbouring one, so an infestation isn't a sign of poor care.

## Treating without synthetic chemicals

1. **Isolate the plant.** Check every plant that was near it, especially succulents, which mealybugs like.
2. **Cut off badly infested or dead growth.** Bag it and put it in the rubbish, not the compost.
3. **Remove the bugs** with tweezers, a cotton bud dipped in 70% isopropyl alcohol, or a strong spray of water.
4. **Clean off honeydew** from the plant, pot, saucer and nearby surfaces.
5. **Check the roots.** Take the plant out of its pot. If there's white fluff on the roots, rinse them, repot in fresh mix and scrub the pot.
6. **Repeat every 5–7 days for about 4 weeks.** Eggs hidden in the fluff hatch after the first clean-up, so one clean check isn't the end.
7. **Return the plant to the others** only after two clean checks in a row.

Milder optional extras:

- Insecticidal (potassium) soap. Patch-test it first.
- Outdoors, ladybirds and lacewings eat mealybugs, so don't spray where they're active.

## Cautions for ferns

- Ferns burn easily. Oil sprays, including "natural" ones like neem and white oil, and some soaps can damage the fronds, and many labels say not to use them on ferns. Test on one frond and wait 2 days.
- Alcohol can burn fern fronds too. Dab it on the bugs instead of soaking the frond, and expect a little browning.
- Hairy fern stems hide bugs. Tweezers or a soft toothbrush can reach them.
- Ferns regrow from the crown, so cutting off most of the fronds is fine. If the crown itself is infested, replacing the plant is usually easier.

## When to give up

If the bugs keep coming back after 3–4 weekly rounds, or the crown or roots are heavily infested, throw the plant out in a sealed bag to protect your other plants.
